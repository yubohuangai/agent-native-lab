The Lab Management Workflow Revolution

How a research lab can become agent-native — starting from one agent that already works.

Yubo Huang · June 2026

Every research lab carries a steady load of operational work: the small, recurring tasks that keep a lab running smoothly. It is necessary work, and someone always does it — but it is repetitive, it never quite ends, and it tends to pull time and attention away from research itself.

I would like to propose a direction for easing it: making our lab agent-native — gradually handing this routine work to AI agents we interact with in plain English. We already have a first agent running, so this is less a leap than a natural next step.

1   The friction in our current workflow

Our equipment inventory is a good place to see the issue. It lives in a shared Google Sheet that we all edit by hand. That works, but it is delicate: a single stray edit can remove a row, with no easy way to recover it and no history to look back on. Everything sits in one tab, so answering "where is the stopwatch?" means scrolling and searching. And keeping it tidy is a recurring manual task.

Inventory is simply the clearest case. A similar friction shows up elsewhere — a website that slowly falls out of date, meeting times settled over many messages, papers and slides spread across Drive and chat with no shared memory, server details that live mostly in people's heads. The common thread is that the information is manual to maintain and not always easy to find, and that small overhead quietly adds up across the lab.

2   An implemented starting point: the Lab Inventory Agent

Rather than only describe the idea, I built it — and it is already in use. It is called Atlas, and it looks after our equipment inventory.

Atlas is a Slack agent powered by a self-hosted language model — Qwen3-14B, running locally on Machine 41 — so the information we give it stays within the lab. Every change it makes is saved as a single git commit, which means there is a complete history to review and any mistake can be undone in one step. The fragile spreadsheet is no longer the system of record.

In practice, you simply talk to it. You can tell it you are taking a camera home and it records the loan; ask where the stopwatch is and it answers; mention that you bought a new lens and it adds it. If something is wrong, you say so in plain English and it corrects it. There is nothing to learn first.

3   A blueprint for the future

Atlas covers one kind of task, but the same approach could ease others. A few that come to mind: organizing journal-club papers and slides into a searchable, lab-wide knowledge base; helping schedule meetings and track who can attend; keeping the website up to date; answering everyday questions about our servers. These are examples rather than a fixed plan — the list could be shorter or longer, and it will grow as we learn what is genuinely useful.

One reason this feels practical rather than aspirational: the infrastructure behind Atlas is built once and then shared by every agent that follows.

4   Maintenance, and growing together

The real promise of an agent-native lab is not only convenience; it is how little upkeep it asks for. Today, much of our operational work depends on a particular person remembering to do it. When agents absorb that work, what remains is light enough for anyone to handle — the maintenance burden moves toward near-zero, and it no longer rests on one set of shoulders.

There is a second effect I find even more compelling. A spreadsheet is exactly as useful on day 500 as on day 1. An agent is different: it remembers every change and everything we tell it, so the lab's shared knowledge accumulates over time instead of slipping away.

Our agents and humans grow together — the more we use them, the more useful they become.
