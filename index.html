The Lab Management Workflow Revolution

How our lab could become agent-native — and why Atlas, our first AI agent, is only the beginning.

Yubo Huang · June 2026 · for Prof. Cheng & Dr. Zhang

Every lab runs on a layer of small, manual chores, and ours is no exception. Someone keeps the website from going stale. Someone schedules the weekly meeting and chases the no-shows. Someone collects the journal-club papers and slides, and someone decides who presents next. Someone tracks where the equipment is, and someone fields the daily "which GPU is free?" question. None of this is hard. None of it is interesting. And that is exactly the problem: it is mindless work, it never ends, and nobody really wants to own it.

I think we can do better. I want to propose that we make our lab agent-native — that we hand these chores, one by one, to AI agents we simply talk to in plain English. Not someday; starting now. We already have the first one running.

The chores nobody wants to do

Take our equipment inventory. It lives in a shared Google Sheet that we all edit by hand, and it is fragile in every way a spreadsheet is fragile. One stray edit deletes a row, with no history to roll back to. Everything is crammed into a single tab, so finding "where is the stopwatch?" means scrolling and Ctrl-F. And every update is a manual chore.

The inventory is not special — it is just the clearest example. The same story repeats across the lab: the website that drifts out of date, the meeting times negotiated over a dozen messages, the papers scattered between Drive and chat with no shared memory, the server details that live only in people's heads. Five chores, one pattern: fragile, hard to search, and entirely manual. Worse, the busywork quietly wears down our communication and collaboration — because nobody enjoys being the person who has to do it.

What "agent-native" means

Agent-native means the default way to get something done is to have a conversation. You do not open a spreadsheet or edit a template; you tell an agent what you need, in plain English, in a Slack channel. Three things change once that becomes the norm:

  • Plain English replaces forms and files. If you can chat, you can run lab operations — no onboarding, no training.
  • Maintenance trends toward zero. The agent does the upkeep; whatever is left is light enough for anyone to do.
  • Humans and agents grow together. Every interaction adds to the lab's shared memory, so the agents become more useful the more we use them.

That last point is the one I find most exciting. A spreadsheet is exactly as useful on day 500 as on day 1. An agent that records everything it does — and everything we tell it — keeps getting better.

We already have proof: Atlas

This is not hypothetical. I have built the first agent, and it is running today. Its name is Atlas, and it manages our equipment inventory.

Atlas is a Slack agent powered by a self-hosted language model — Qwen3-14B, running locally on Machine 41 — so nothing we tell it ever leaves the lab. Every change it makes is recorded as a single git commit, which means the entire history is an audit log and any mistake can be undone with one command. The fragile Google Sheet, where a careless edit could quietly erase a row forever, is gone.

And you really do just talk to it. Tell it you are taking a camera home and it logs the loan; ask where the stopwatch is and it tells you; mention that you bought a new lens and it adds the lens to the inventory. If you get something wrong, you say so in plain English and it fixes it. There is nothing to learn.

Five chores, five agents

Atlas is one agent, but the recipe behind it — a Slack channel, a private language model, and tools whose every action is recorded in git — is general. The same approach fits every chore on the list:

  • Inventory (Atlas). Running now: borrow, return, locate, and add equipment, all by talking to it.
  • Papers and journal club (a "lab-brain" agent). I have designed this one. Each presenter uploads their paper and their slides; the agent ingests both into a lab-wide knowledge base we can actually search — "what did we read about diffusion models last term?" It can also play lab manager: coordinate the presentation order and let people request leave in Slack when they cannot make their turn. We would no longer need a human to own the rotation.
  • Lab meetings (a scheduler). Proposes times, sends reminders, tracks attendance, and reshuffles automatically when someone cannot make it.
  • The website (a steward). "Add my new paper," "update my bio" — the agent edits the site and the change is versioned, so the page never drifts out of date.
  • Servers (an ops agent). This one has a head start: our inventory already tracks the lab's machines. "Which GPU is free on Machine 41? What is the IP for the NAS?" — answered in plain English instead of a group-chat scavenger hunt.

Only the first exists today. The rest are where I would like us to go.

It is all one pattern

The important thing is that these are not five separate projects. They are five instances of a single pattern: a person talks to an agent in Slack, the agent uses tools to do the work, and every change lands in a git-audited record. The hard part — the private model server, the Slack integration, the audit convention — is built once. Each new agent is then mostly a matter of new tools, not new plumbing, so the cost of the next agent keeps falling. That is what makes "agent-native" a direction we can actually walk, not a slogan.

Why I think it is worth it

A few reasons this feels right for us, beyond convenience. It is private by design — a self-hosted model means our data stays on our own hardware. It is safe — git history is the source of truth, and mistakes revert in one command. It is nearly free to maintain — the upkeep that used to need a dedicated person is absorbed by the agents. And it is good for us as researchers: building and living with agents is hands-on experience with exactly where the field is heading. The lab gets to practice the future, not just read about it.

Where this goes

Atlas may be the lab's first AI agent. With your support, I do not think it will be the last.

Our agents and humans will grow together — the more we use them, the smarter they get.

I would genuinely love your thoughts: whether the direction makes sense, which chore you would most like to see disappear next, and what you would want an agent to do for you. This is the start of a conversation, not a finished plan.
